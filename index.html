01 /THE PRINCIPLE

The thread through the work

Reality gets the final vote.

I started by building rockets with a small team in New Jersey. A sequence of failed test flights forced us to change the launch system; the redesign helped carry us to the American Rocketry Challenge nationals. At Embry-Riddle, I moved from student flight hardware to liquid-engine ground control and propulsion development.

The same question follows me into professional test engineering: what do we actually know, and what would change our minds? I like work where a clear procedure, a well-placed sensor, or one teammate speaking up can change the decision before it becomes a failure.

01 / GROUND CONTROL

PROJECT TRITON / ERPL · 2024–25

Make the system legible when the stakes rise.

The problem. A liquid-engine test concentrates sensor data, valve commands, and rapid decisions into a small crew at the console. The interface has to make state obvious while the hardware is changing.

My work. I developed ERPL ground-control software with live pressure, temperature, and load telemetry, remote valve actuation, sequencing, and fault handling. I integrated data acquisition and served as ground controller for more than eight waterflow and static-fire tests at Cecil Spaceport.

What happened. Project Triton completed ethanol/LOX hot-fire tests in 2025. That result belongs to the whole ERPL team; my contribution was the controls and operating role described here.

OPERATING PRINCIPLE If the operator cannot tell what the system is doing, the system is not ready to run.

02 / PROPULSION

REGENERATIVE ENGINE / ERPL

Turn a stalled design into a buildable one.

Constraint: thermal, injector, structural, and performance choices had to agree before hardware could move forward. As project lead, I rebuilt the Python analysis workflow so the team could compare those trades in one loop.

The workflow enabled more than 1,000 design iterations and helped move the project into fabrication. Status: fabrication; this engine has not fired. A photo of engine hardware here documents ERPL work, not a test of this specific design.

03 / THE TEAM

LAB LEADERSHIP / ERPL

Make the team ready, too.

As president of ERPL, a student propulsion lab with about 180 members, I worked across technical teams, design reviews, test readiness, sponsors, and operations. The visible event is a hot fire; most of the work is the quieter coordination that lets the right people make a safe, informed call.

I learned to make room for disagreement early, document decisions, and treat readiness as a shared responsibility.

03 /WHERE IT STARTED

ResistoJets Rocketry · New Jersey · 2021–24

A setback can be useful data.

43ftfrom the target altitude at the 2024 NASA Student Launch competition

I founded a student rocket team with friends, then learned how much team building, documentation, and iteration sit behind a successful flight. Early test failures and a thrust-to-weight problem forced us to redesign our launch setup. A custom 10-foot rail helped get us to nationals in the American Rocketry Challenge, where we finished 17th of more than 800 teams.

The next season, ResistoJets won the NASA Student Launch Altitude Award. The rocket finished 43 feet from a 3,900-foot target. More than the result, I remember the reviews, recovery work, and people who made another attempt possible.

2025 / RESEARCH

Embry-Riddle CARS & Space Robotics

Research assistant · resilient aerospace and autonomous systems

At the Center for Aerospace Resilient Systems, I built a pipeline that analyzed noisy flight-simulator logs in real time and returned cyber-event cues to the simulated crew. The team demonstrated the system at DEF CON 33 Aerospace Village. I also worked on offline vulnerability analysis, and at the Space Robotics and Generative Estimation Lab I studied GNSS-spoofing detection and robotic fault monitoring. The common challenge: make imperfect data useful to a human operator.

05 /HOW I WORK

A working method

Prepare carefully.
Stay correctable.

01 / BEFORE

Define the decision.

Identify what the test must prove, what could invalidate the data, and what conditions require a stop.

02 / DURING

Make state visible.

Give operators clear telemetry, explicit commands, and room to question a reading or a sequence.

03 / AFTER

Follow the evidence.

Compare expectation with measurement, document the gap, and change the design or procedure before repeating.

I am aiming for test, ground-systems, and launch-operations work where that discipline matters. Over time, I hope to earn responsibility in launch control. The projects on this page are the work I can speak to today.
